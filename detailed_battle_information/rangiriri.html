Battle of Rangiriri

The battle of Rangiriri on 20 November was one of the deadliest conflicts of the New Zealand Land Wars. General Cameron, commanding over 1,400 British troops, attacked Rangiriri fortifications, which consisted of a kilometre-long line of trenches and earthworks designed by Te Wharepu. The Māori defenders, numbering about 500, resisted fiercely. Despite a two-hour bombardment and repeated assaults, British forces faced heavy casualties, losing around 110 men. They managed to breach parts of the line but failed to capture the central redoubt. The Māori garrison raised a white flag, intending to negotiate. However, the British took it as a sign of surrender, capturing 183 Māori warriors. Belich noted that the surrender might have been influenced by British misuse of the flag of truce, and argued that Māori forces might have continued resisting or awaited reinforcements. The battle cost Māori forces 35 lives and marked a significant defeat in the Waikato wars. The prisoners, initially held on a coal hulk, later escaped in 1864.

Battle of Rangiriri

Date: 20–21 November 1863

Location: Rangiriri, Waikato, New Zealand

Coordinates: 37°26′00″S 175°06′00″E

Result: British victory

Belligerents

United Kingdom: New Zealand colonial forces

Māori:Māori King Movement of Waikato

Strength

United Kingdom: 1420 British regulars, 3 cannons, 2 gunboats

Māori: 500 warriors

Casualties and Losses

United Kingdom: 49 killed, 87 wounded

Māori: 36 killed, 35 wounded, 183 taken prisoner

How it has been Redressed

In 2009 the New Zealand Transport Agency, which is responsible for the Waikato Expressway Rangiriri Bypass roading project, began working in partnership with Waikato-Tainui to restore the Rangiriri Pā site. The Rangiriri Bypass and restoration works were scheduled for completion by the 150th anniversary of the Battle of Rangiriri, in November 2013 but was not complete until 2017. As part of the Rangiriri Bypass project, the Rangiriri Pā site was restored and the old State Highway 1 that cut through the pā was removed. As part of the Rangiriri Bypass Project, the site was the subject of an archeological dig in mid-2011. The site was returned to Waikato-Tainui by the Minister of Conservation Hon Maggie Barry on 19 August 2016 at a ceremony at Tūrangawaewae Marae during the Māori King Tuheitia 10th Coronation anniversary.
